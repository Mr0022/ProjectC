# The HAR-RV Model: Methodological Specification

**Raw (levels) and logarithmic formulations, with explicit index conventions**

Reference implementation: [`HAR-RV_RUN.PY`](../HAR-RV_RUN.PY) (raw scale by default, log scale under `--log`).
Every equation below is numbered and matches the code line-for-line; the correspondence is
tabulated in [Section 15](#15-correspondence-between-notation-and-code).

Primary source: Corsi, F. (2009), *A Simple Approximate Long-Memory Model of Realized
Volatility*, **Journal of Financial Econometrics** 7(2), 174–196.

---

## 1. Notation and timing conventions

Let $t = 1, \dots, T$ index trading days. Within day $t$ the log price is sampled on an
intraday grid of $M$ intervals (e.g. $M = 288$ for 5-minute sampling of a 24-hour FX day),

$$
p_{t,0} < p_{t,1} < \dots < p_{t,M},
\qquad
r_{t,j} \;=\; p_{t,j} - p_{t,j-1}, \quad j = 1,\dots,M .
\tag{1}
$$

$\mathcal{F}_t$ denotes the information set generated by all data observed **up to and
including the close of day $t$**.

### 1.1 The two index conventions — state which one you use

This is the single most common source of confusion in applied HAR work, because the two
conventions produce *identical* regressions but *different-looking* equations. Both are
correct; papers must declare one.

| | **Convention A — origin-indexed** (Corsi 2009; used in this document) | **Convention B — target-indexed** (used by the dataframe rows in `HAR-RV_RUN.PY`) |
|---|---|---|
| Row label $t$ means | last day of the information set | first day of the forecast window |
| Regressors | dated $t$, use $RV_t, \dots, RV_{t-21}$ | dated $t$, use $RV_{t-1}, \dots, RV_{t-22}$ |
| Target | $RV_{t,t+h}$ spans days $t+1,\dots,t+h$ | $Y_t^{(h)}$ spans days $t,\dots,t+h-1$ |
| Map | $t_{\mathrm{A}} = t_{\mathrm{B}} - 1$ | $t_{\mathrm{B}} = t_{\mathrm{A}} + 1$ |

Under either convention the information set ends **strictly one day before the first day of
the target window**, so the model is a genuine out-of-sample projection with no look-ahead.
The rest of this document uses **Convention A**. Write in a paper:

> All regressors are measured on the information set $\mathcal{F}_t$ and the dependent
> variable spans days $t+1$ through $t+h$; no contemporaneous information enters the
> right-hand side.

## 2. The volatility measure

Daily **realized variance** is the sum of squared intraday returns (Andersen & Bollerslev,
1998):

$$
RV_t \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^{M} r_{t,j}^{2} .
\tag{2}
$$

Under standard no-arbitrage assumptions with a semimartingale log price
$dp_s = \mu_s\,ds + \sigma_s\,dW_s + dJ_s$,

$$
RV_t \;\xrightarrow[M \to \infty]{p}\;
\underbrace{\int_{t-1}^{t} \sigma_s^{2}\,ds}_{\text{integrated variance } IV_t}
\;+\;
\sum_{t-1 < s \le t} \big(\Delta J_s\big)^{2},
\tag{3}
$$

i.e. $RV_t$ is a consistent estimator of the **quadratic variation** $QV_t$, which equals
$IV_t$ in the absence of jumps.

**Modelling object.** The HAR is specified on the *variance* $RV_t$, not on the standard
deviation $\sqrt{RV_t}$. State the units explicitly (e.g. daily percentage-squared, or
annualised by $\times 252$); all coefficients except $\beta_0$ are scale-free, so only the
intercept and the loss levels change under rescaling — see §12.3.

**Positivity.** $RV_t > 0$ is required throughout: $\ln RV_t$ is otherwise undefined and
QLIKE is undefined. Non-positive entries are exchange holidays with no trading, not
genuine zero-variance days, and are removed from the sample before any regressor is built.

## 3. Heterogeneous components (the regressors)

The HAR summarises the past by three backward-looking averages of increasing horizon —
the *daily*, *weekly* ($5$ trading days) and *monthly* ($22$ trading days) components:

$$
RV_t^{(d)} \;=\; RV_t ,
\tag{4}
$$

$$
RV_t^{(w)} \;=\; \frac{1}{5}\sum_{i=0}^{4} RV_{t-i}
\;=\; \frac{1}{5}\big(RV_t + RV_{t-1} + RV_{t-2} + RV_{t-3} + RV_{t-4}\big),
\tag{5}
$$

$$
RV_t^{(m)} \;=\; \frac{1}{22}\sum_{i=0}^{21} RV_{t-i} .
\tag{6}
$$

Note two properties that must be reported honestly:

1. **The windows are nested, not orthogonal.** $\{t\} \subset \{t,\dots,t-4\} \subset
   \{t,\dots,t-21\}$, so the three regressors are strongly positively correlated
   (typically $\rho > 0.7$). Coefficients are therefore *partial* effects conditional on
   the other two averages and should not be read as "the effect of last week's
   volatility". Report the correlation matrix and a VIF or condition number.
2. **Windows are in trading days, not calendar days.** $5$ and $22$ are conventions
   approximating a week and a month; they are not estimated. Robustness to
   $(5,22) \to (5,20)$ or $(5,10,22)$ is a standard referee request.

### 3.1 Economic motivation: the partial volatility cascade

Corsi (2009) derives (4)–(6) from a hierarchy of market participants with different
investment horizons. Writing $\sigma^{(\cdot)}$ for the latent partial volatility of each
horizon and letting long-horizon agents' expectations feed down into shorter horizons:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sigma^{(m)}_{t+1m} &= c^{(m)} + \phi^{(m)} RV^{(m)}_{t} + \tilde{\omega}^{(m)}_{t+1m}, \\[2pt]
\sigma^{(w)}_{t+1w} &= c^{(w)} + \phi^{(w)} RV^{(w)}_{t}
                       + \gamma^{(w)} \mathbb{E}_t\!\big[\sigma^{(m)}_{t+1m}\big]
                       + \tilde{\omega}^{(w)}_{t+1w}, \\[2pt]
\sigma^{(d)}_{t+1d} &= c^{(d)} + \phi^{(d)} RV^{(d)}_{t}
                       + \gamma^{(d)} \mathbb{E}_t\!\big[\sigma^{(w)}_{t+1w}\big]
                       + \tilde{\omega}^{(d)}_{t+1d}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{7}
$$

Recursive substitution of the top two equations into the third, together with the
measurement equation $RV_{t+1} = \sigma^{(d)}_{t+1d} + \omega_{t+1}$, yields the
three-factor stochastic volatility model that the HAR regression (9) approximates. The
model is not a formal long-memory process; it *mimics* hyperbolic autocorrelation decay by
superposing three exponentially-decaying components, which is why the title of the source
paper says "approximate long-memory".

---

## 4. The multi-horizon dependent variable

For forecast horizon $h \in \{1, 5, 22\}$ define the **$h$-day forward average realized
variance**

$$
RV_{t,\,t+h} \;=\; \frac{1}{h}\sum_{i=1}^{h} RV_{t+i},
\qquad h \ge 1 ,
\tag{8}
$$

which spans days $t+1, \dots, t+h$ and collapses to $RV_{t,\,t+1} = RV_{t+1}$ at $h=1$.

**Average, not sum.** Using $\sum_{i=1}^{h} RV_{t+i}$ instead multiplies the dependent
variable, all four coefficients and every residual by $h$, hence MSE by $h^2$. $R^2$ is
invariant, but reported loss levels are not, and horizons cease to be comparable. Equation
(8) is Corsi's convention and the one used here.

## 5. Specification I — raw (levels) HAR-RV

$$
\boxed{\;
RV_{t,\,t+h}
\;=\;
\beta_0
+ \beta_d\, RV_t^{(d)}
+ \beta_w\, RV_t^{(w)}
+ \beta_m\, RV_t^{(m)}
+ \varepsilon^{(h)}_{t+h},
\qquad t = 22, \dots, T-h .
\;}
\tag{9}
$$

with $\mathbb{E}\big[\varepsilon^{(h)}_{t+h} \mid \mathcal{F}_t\big] = 0$.

**Effective sample.** The monthly component requires $RV_{t-21}$, so estimation starts at
$t = 22$; the target requires $RV_{t+h}$, so it ends at $t = T-h$. Hence

$$
T_h \;=\; T - h - 21
\tag{10}
$$

usable observations. Report $T_h$ per horizon — it differs across $h$ and a common
mistake is quoting the raw sample length $T$.

**Interpretation.** $\beta_d, \beta_w, \beta_m$ are the marginal contributions of the
short-, medium- and long-run components; $\beta_d + \beta_w + \beta_m$ measures total
persistence (§12.1). $\beta_0$ carries the units of $RV$.

**Known limitations, to be stated rather than hidden.** In levels the residuals are
severely heteroskedastic (residual dispersion scales with the level of $RV$) and strongly
right-skewed and leptokurtic; the fit is dominated by a handful of crisis observations;
and the fitted value is not constrained to be positive, so $\widehat{RV} \le 0$ can occur
in quiet periods. These are exactly the pathologies the log specification addresses.

## 6. Specification II — logarithmic HAR-RV

### 6.1 The log-of-mean convention

Define the log components and log target by taking the logarithm **outside** every sum:

$$
x_t^{(d)} \;=\; \ln RV_t ,
\qquad
x_t^{(w)} \;=\; \ln\!\left( \frac{1}{5}\sum_{i=0}^{4} RV_{t-i} \right),
\qquad
x_t^{(m)} \;=\; \ln\!\left( \frac{1}{22}\sum_{i=0}^{21} RV_{t-i} \right),
\tag{11}
$$

$$
y_{t,\,t+h} \;=\; \ln RV_{t,\,t+h} \;=\; \ln\!\left( \frac{1}{h}\sum_{i=1}^{h} RV_{t+i} \right),
\tag{12}
$$

$$
\boxed{\;
y_{t,\,t+h}
\;=\;
\beta_0
+ \beta_d\, x_t^{(d)}
+ \beta_w\, x_t^{(w)}
+ \beta_m\, x_t^{(m)}
+ u^{(h)}_{t+h},
\qquad t = 22, \dots, T-h .
\;}
\tag{13}
$$

Equivalently $x_t^{(\cdot)} = \ln RV_t^{(\cdot)}$ and $y_{t,t+h} = \ln RV_{t,t+h}$: the log
specification regresses the log of each *arithmetic* average on the log of the others.
Coefficients are **elasticities** — a $1\%$ rise in the weekly average raises the expected
$h$-day forward average by $\beta_w\%$.

### 6.2 Log-of-mean versus mean-of-logs (Jensen)

The two aggregation orders are different regressors and must not be conflated. For any
window of length $n$, Jensen's inequality on the concave $\ln(\cdot)$ gives

$$
\underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \ln RV_{t-i}}_{\ln(\text{geometric mean})}
\;\;\le\;\;
\underbrace{\ln\!\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} RV_{t-i}\right)}_{\ln(\text{arithmetic mean})},
\tag{14}
$$

with equality if and only if $RV$ is constant over the window. The gap widens with the
dispersion of $RV$ inside the window, so it is largest precisely in turbulent periods.

Both conventions appear in the literature. **This implementation uses log-of-mean, (11)–(12).**
The decisive property is the exact identity

$$
\exp\!\big( y_{t,\,t+h} \big) \;=\; RV_{t,\,t+h}
\qquad \text{for every } h ,
\tag{15}
$$

so the log model and the levels model target the *same* object at every horizon, and
back-transformed losses are directly comparable. Under mean-of-logs, $\exp(y)$ would be a
geometric forward mean — a smaller and mechanically smoother quantity — and cross-scale
loss comparisons would be honest only at $h = 1$, where the two means coincide.

At $h = 1$ and for the daily component ($n = 1$) the distinction is vacuous: a window of
one has nothing to aggregate, so $x_t^{(d)} = \ln RV_t$ under either convention.

**Sums versus means are cosmetic on the log scale**, because
$\ln\!\big(\sum_{i} RV_{t-i}\big) = \ln\!\big(\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_i RV_{t-i}\big) + \ln n$
and the constant is absorbed by $\beta_0$. Only the intercept changes; slopes, residuals
and $R^2$ do not.

### 6.3 Back-transformation and the Jensen correction

A log-scale forecast must be mapped back to a variance before it can be scored by QLIKE
or compared with a levels forecast. Because $\exp(\cdot)$ is convex,
$\exp\big(\mathbb{E}[\,y \mid \mathcal{F}_t\,]\big)$ is the conditional **median**, not the
mean, and understates it systematically. Under conditional log-normality of the errors,
$u^{(h)}_{t+h}\mid\mathcal{F}_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_u^2)$,

$$
\mathbb{E}\big[\,RV_{t,\,t+h} \mid \mathcal{F}_t \,\big]
\;=\;
\exp\!\left( \mathbb{E}\big[\,y_{t,\,t+h}\mid\mathcal{F}_t\,\big] + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma_u^{2} \right),
\tag{16}
$$

which is implemented as the smearing-type estimator

$$
\widehat{RV}_{t,\,t+h}
\;=\;
\exp\!\left( \hat{y}_{t,\,t+h} + \tfrac{1}{2}\hat{\sigma}_u^{2} \right),
\qquad
\hat{\sigma}_u^{2} \;=\; \frac{1}{T_h - K}\sum_{t} \hat{u}_t^{2},
\tag{17}
$$

with $\hat{\sigma}_u^2$ the **in-sample (training) residual variance** and $K = 4$ the
number of estimated coefficients. The correction is exact under Gaussian log residuals and
a good approximation otherwise (Andersen, Bollerslev, Diebold & Labys, 2003). It is a
level shift, not noise: it does not average out over the evaluation sample.

Two refinements worth a footnote in a paper. (i) A fully conditional version adds the
parameter-estimation variance, $\exp\!\big(\hat{y} + \tfrac{1}{2}[\hat{\sigma}_u^2 +
z_t'\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat\beta) z_t]\big)$, which is $O(T_h^{-1})$ and negligible in
samples of a few thousand days. (ii) If normality is doubtful, Duan's (1983) nonparametric
smearing estimator $\widehat{RV} = \exp(\hat y)\cdot T_h^{-1}\sum_t \exp(\hat u_t)$ replaces
$\exp(\hat\sigma_u^2/2)$ and requires no distributional assumption.

By construction $\widehat{RV}_{t,t+h} > 0$, so the log specification cannot produce the
negative variance forecasts that (9) can.

## 7. Estimation

### 7.1 OLS in matrix form

Stack the regressors as $z_t = \big(1,\; RV_t^{(d)},\; RV_t^{(w)},\; RV_t^{(m)}\big)'$ (raw)
or $z_t = \big(1,\; x_t^{(d)},\; x_t^{(w)},\; x_t^{(m)}\big)'$ (log), $K = \dim(z_t) = 4$,
and let $Z$ be the $T_h \times K$ design matrix. Both specifications are linear in
parameters and estimated by ordinary least squares:

$$
\hat\beta^{(h)}
\;=\;
\big(Z'Z\big)^{-1} Z' y
\;=\;
\left(\sum_{t} z_t z_t'\right)^{\!-1} \left(\sum_{t} z_t\, y_{t,\,t+h}\right).
\tag{18}
$$

A **separate regression is run for each horizon $h$** (direct multi-step projection). The
alternative — estimating the $h=1$ model and iterating it forward — imposes the cascade
recursion and is more efficient if the model is correctly specified, but is more sensitive
to misspecification. Direct projection is Corsi's approach and the one used here; say
which you use.

### 7.2 Overlapping observations induce an MA structure

For $h > 1$ consecutive dependent variables share $h-1$ daily terms:
$RV_{t,t+h}$ and $RV_{t+1,t+h+1}$ both contain $RV_{t+2},\dots,RV_{t+h}$. If the one-day
innovations $\{\nu_t\}$ are serially uncorrelated, the $h$-day error is their moving
average,

$$
\varepsilon^{(h)}_{t+h} \;\approx\; \frac{1}{h}\sum_{i=1}^{h} \nu_{t+i}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\mathrm{Cov}\big( \varepsilon^{(h)}_{t+h},\, \varepsilon^{(h)}_{t+h+j} \big)
= \frac{h - |j|}{h^{2}}\,\sigma_\nu^{2}\;\mathbf{1}\{|j| < h\},
\tag{19}
$$

an $\mathrm{MA}(h-1)$ process. OLS remains **consistent** — the moment condition
$\mathbb{E}[z_t\varepsilon^{(h)}_{t+h}] = 0$ is untouched — but conventional standard
errors are badly understated. Autocorrelation-robust inference is therefore mandatory, not
optional, at $h = 5$ and $h = 22$.

### 7.3 Newey–West HAC covariance

Let $\hat{e}_t$ be the OLS residuals. With the Bartlett kernel and bandwidth $L$,

$$
\hat{S}_L
\;=\;
\sum_{t} \hat{e}_t^{2} z_t z_t'
\;+\;
\sum_{j=1}^{L} w_j \sum_{t=j+1} \hat{e}_t \hat{e}_{t-j}
\big( z_t z_{t-j}' + z_{t-j} z_t' \big),
\qquad
w_j \;=\; 1 - \frac{j}{L+1},
\tag{20}
$$

$$
\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}\big(\hat\beta^{(h)}\big)
\;=\;
\frac{T_h}{T_h - K}\,
\big(Z'Z\big)^{-1}\, \hat{S}_L \,\big(Z'Z\big)^{-1},
\tag{21}
$$

where $T_h/(T_h-K)$ is the finite-sample degrees-of-freedom correction (Newey & West,
1987). The Bartlett weights guarantee $\hat{S}_L$ is positive semi-definite. Reported
$t$-statistics and $p$-values use (21) throughout.

### 7.4 Bandwidth selection

Two rules combine:

$$
L_{\text{overlap}}(h) \;=\; 2(h-1),
\qquad
L_{\text{plug-in}}(T_h) \;=\; \left\lfloor 4\left(\frac{T_h}{100}\right)^{2/9} \right\rfloor,
\tag{22}
$$

$$
L(h, T_h) \;=\; \max\big\{\, L_{\text{overlap}}(h),\; L_{\text{plug-in}}(T_h) \,\big\}.
\tag{23}
$$

$L_{\text{overlap}}$ covers the autocorrelation *mechanically* induced by overlap. Note
that (19) gives an $\mathrm{MA}(h-1)$ error, so $h-1$ is the theoretical minimum; the
factor of two is the conservative convention standard in the realized-volatility
literature (Patton & Sheppard, 2009), and it is what (22) implements. $L_{\text{plug-in}}$
is the Newey–West (1994) data-driven rule, which addresses serial correlation *present in
the series itself*, independently of overlap.

The maximum in (23) matters at $h=1$, where $L_{\text{overlap}} = 0$ would assert
serially uncorrelated residuals — an assertion the Ljung–Box test rejects on realized
volatility data. Bartlett-kernel HAC with $L=0$ retains only the $j=0$ term of (20) and
collapses to White/HC1: robust to heteroskedasticity but *not* a HAC correction. The
plug-in rule therefore takes over at $h=1$. For $T_h \approx 3{,}300$ it returns $L = 8$.
At $h=5$ and $h=22$ the overlap floor ($8$ and $42$) dominates and the conventional
bandwidth is preserved exactly. Report $L$ for every horizon; a robustness column using
the pure $2(h-1)$ rule is cheap and pre-empts the obvious referee question.

## 8. Evaluation

### 8.1 Sample split

The split is chronological, never random — shuffling destroys the temporal ordering and
leaks future information into the estimation sample:

$$
\mathcal{T}_{\text{train}} = \{\, t : \mathrm{year}(t) \le 2024 \,\},
\qquad
\mathcal{T}_{\text{test}} = \{\, t : \mathrm{year}(t) \ge 2025 \,\}.
\tag{24}
$$

Coefficients are estimated **once** on $\mathcal{T}_{\text{train}}$ and held fixed over
$\mathcal{T}_{\text{test}}$ (fixed-scheme forecasting). OLS has no hyperparameters, so no
validation split is required; when benchmarking against models that do (neural networks,
etc.), fold their validation years into the HAR training sample and keep the **test window
identical** so losses are comparable. Say so explicitly — it is the difference between a
like-for-like comparison and a rigged one.

Two caveats to note: (i) with a fixed scheme, forecasts far into the test window use
increasingly stale parameters — a rolling or expanding re-estimation window is the standard
robustness check; (ii) $h-1$ observations at the train/test boundary overlap both samples,
a negligible leak at $h=1$ but worth a sentence at $h=22$.

### 8.2 Loss functions

Let $\sigma^2_t$ be the (unobserved) true conditional variance and $\hat{\sigma}^2_t$ the
forecast, with $RV$ used as the proxy for $\sigma^2_t$. Over $n$ test observations:

$$
\mathrm{MSE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{t} \big( RV_t - \hat{\sigma}^2_t \big)^{2},
\qquad
\mathrm{MAE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{t} \big| RV_t - \hat{\sigma}^2_t \big| ,
\tag{25}
$$

$$
\mathrm{QLIKE}
= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{t}
\left[
\frac{RV_t}{\hat{\sigma}^2_t}
- \ln\!\left(\frac{RV_t}{\hat{\sigma}^2_t}\right)
- 1
\right] \;\ge\; 0 ,
\tag{26}
$$

QLIKE (Patton, 2011) is written here in its normalised form: it is non-negative and equals
zero only for a perfect forecast. It penalises under-prediction of volatility far more
heavily than over-prediction, which is the economically relevant asymmetry for risk
management.

**Robustness to the noisy proxy (report this).** Patton (2011) shows that MSE and QLIKE
are *robust* loss functions: because $RV_t$ is a conditionally unbiased proxy for
$\sigma^2_t$, the ranking of forecasts by expected loss under the proxy coincides with the
ranking under the true latent variance. **MAE is not robust** — it can reverse the true
ranking. MAE may be reported as a descriptive statistic, but model selection should rest
on MSE and QLIKE.

**Scale of evaluation.** In raw mode all three losses are computed on the $RV$ scale
directly. In log mode, MSE and MAE are reported in $\ln RV$ units (the scale the model is
fitted on, where squared error is a relative-error criterion and is not dominated by a
handful of spikes), while QLIKE requires variances and is computed after back-transforming
by (17). Because of identity (15), $\exp(y_{t,t+h})$ *is* the raw-mode target, so
back-transformed MSE and MAE are directly comparable across the two specifications at
every horizon. Report both the corrected and the naive ($\exp(\hat y)$, no Jensen term)
QLIKE so the size of the correction is visible rather than taken on trust.

**Non-positive forecasts.** Levels OLS can return $\hat{\sigma}^2_t \le 0$, where QLIKE is
undefined. Such forecasts are floored at $\varsigma \cdot \overline{RV}_{\text{train}}$
with $\varsigma = 10^{-4}$, and the count and percentage are reported. Discarding them
instead would delete the *worst* forecasts and bias QLIKE downward — a silent
specification advantage for the levels model. The floor never binds in log mode, since
(17) is strictly positive.

### 8.3 Forecast comparison

Pairwise comparison uses Diebold–Mariano (1995) on the loss differential
$d_t = L(RV_t, \hat{\sigma}^2_{1,t}) - L(RV_t, \hat{\sigma}^2_{2,t})$:

$$
\mathrm{DM} \;=\; \frac{\bar{d}}{\sqrt{\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}_{\mathrm{HAC}}(\bar{d})}}
\;\xrightarrow{d}\; \mathcal{N}(0,1),
\qquad
\bar{d} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{t} d_t ,
\tag{27}
$$

with the HAC variance computed under the same bandwidth rule (23) — the loss differential
inherits the same $\mathrm{MA}(h-1)$ overlap. For more than two competing models, the
Model Confidence Set (Hansen, Lunde & Nason, 2011) controls the multiple-comparison problem
and is preferable to a grid of pairwise DM tests.

## 9. Residual diagnostics

Computed on the training residuals of each horizon, reported per specification:

| Test | Statistic | Null hypothesis | Expected on RV data |
|---|---|---|---|
| Durbin–Watson | $DW = \sum_{t=2}(\hat{e}_t-\hat{e}_{t-1})^2 / \sum_t \hat{e}_t^2$ | no first-order autocorrelation | $\ll 2$ for $h>1$ (mechanical overlap) |
| Ljung–Box | $Q(L) = T_h(T_h+2)\sum_{j=1}^{L} \hat{\rho}_j^2/(T_h-j) \sim \chi^2_L$ | no autocorrelation up to lag $L$ | rejects, even at $h=1$ |
| Jarque–Bera | $JB = \tfrac{T_h}{6}\big(S^2 + \tfrac{1}{4}(\kappa-3)^2\big) \sim \chi^2_2$ | normality | rejects strongly in levels; improves sharply in logs |
| Breusch–Pagan | $LM = T_h R^2_{\text{aux}} \sim \chi^2_{K-1}$ | homoskedasticity | very large in levels; falls by orders of magnitude in logs |

The diagnostics are not a pass/fail gate — they are the *justification* for the HAC
covariance and for the log transform. A large Breusch–Pagan statistic in levels is
expected, not a coding error: it confirms that classical OLS standard errors would be
understated and that (21) is doing necessary work. Comparing the two specifications'
diagnostics is the cleanest evidence of what the log transform buys: it converts
multiplicative volatility-of-volatility into approximately additive noise, but it does
**not** remove serial correlation, so HAC remains necessary in both.

## 10. Forecast construction

Given $\hat\beta^{(h)}$ estimated on the training sample, the forecast made at the close of
day $t$ for the average variance over days $t+1,\dots,t+h$ is

$$
\widehat{RV}_{t,\,t+h}
= \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_d RV_t^{(d)} + \hat\beta_w RV_t^{(w)} + \hat\beta_m RV_t^{(m)}
\qquad \text{(raw)},
\tag{28}
$$

$$
\hat{y}_{t,\,t+h}
= \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_d x_t^{(d)} + \hat\beta_w x_t^{(w)} + \hat\beta_m x_t^{(m)},
\qquad
\widehat{RV}_{t,\,t+h} = \exp\!\left(\hat{y}_{t,\,t+h} + \tfrac{1}{2}\hat\sigma_u^2\right)
\qquad \text{(log)} .
\tag{29}
$$

Annualised realized volatility, if that is the reported quantity, is
$\widehat{\mathrm{Vol}} = \sqrt{252 \cdot \widehat{RV}_{t,t+h}}$ — apply the square root
**after** the Jensen correction, and note that $\sqrt{\cdot}$ introduces its own (opposite,
concave) Jensen gap if a conditional-mean volatility rather than a conditional-mean
variance is wanted.

## 11. Model summary in one block (for a paper)

> **Raw.** For $h \in \{1,5,22\}$ and $t = 22,\dots,T-h$,
> $$RV_{t,t+h} = \beta_0 + \beta_d RV^{(d)}_t + \beta_w RV^{(w)}_t + \beta_m RV^{(m)}_t + \varepsilon^{(h)}_{t+h},$$
> where $RV_{t,t+h} = h^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{h} RV_{t+i}$, $RV^{(d)}_t = RV_t$,
> $RV^{(w)}_t = 5^{-1}\sum_{i=0}^{4} RV_{t-i}$ and $RV^{(m)}_t = 22^{-1}\sum_{i=0}^{21} RV_{t-i}$.
>
> **Log.** The same equation in $y_{t,t+h} = \ln RV_{t,t+h}$ and
> $x^{(\cdot)}_t = \ln RV^{(\cdot)}_t$, i.e. the logarithm is applied to each arithmetic
> average, not to the individual daily terms. Level forecasts follow from
> $\widehat{RV} = \exp(\hat y + \hat\sigma_u^2/2)$.
>
> Both are estimated by OLS with Newey–West standard errors, bandwidth
> $L = \max\{2(h-1), \lfloor 4(T_h/100)^{2/9}\rfloor\}$, to account for the
> $\mathrm{MA}(h-1)$ error induced by the overlapping dependent variable.

## 12. Analytical properties worth reporting

### 12.1 Implied AR(22) representation and persistence

At $h=1$ the HAR is a *constrained* AR(22). Collecting terms in (9),
$RV_{t+1} = \beta_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{22} \phi_j RV_{t-j+1} + \varepsilon_{t+1}$ with

$$
\phi_1 = \beta_d + \frac{\beta_w}{5} + \frac{\beta_m}{22},
\qquad
\phi_j = \frac{\beta_w}{5} + \frac{\beta_m}{22} \;\; (j = 2,\dots,5),
\qquad
\phi_j = \frac{\beta_m}{22} \;\; (j = 6,\dots,22).
\tag{30}
$$

Twenty-two lags are captured by three free parameters — this parsimony is the model's main
practical advantage over an unrestricted AR(22) or a fractionally-integrated ARFIMA. The
step-function shape of (30) is what generates the slowly-decaying, quasi-hyperbolic
autocorrelation.

Total persistence and the implied unconditional mean:

$$
\sum_{j=1}^{22}\phi_j \;=\; \beta_d + \beta_w + \beta_m ,
\qquad
\mathbb{E}[RV] \;=\; \frac{\beta_0}{1 - \beta_d - \beta_w - \beta_m}.
\tag{31}
$$

Covariance stationarity requires the roots of $1 - \sum_{j=1}^{22}\phi_j L^j$ to lie
outside the unit circle; when $\beta_d,\beta_w,\beta_m \ge 0$ (the empirically usual case,
so all $\phi_j \ge 0$) this reduces to the simple condition
$\beta_d + \beta_w + \beta_m < 1$. Values just below unity ($0.95$–$0.99$) are typical and
are the model's rendering of near-long-memory. Report the sum with its HAC standard error,
obtained from $\mathrm{Var}(\hat\beta_d + \hat\beta_w + \hat\beta_m) = c'\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\hat\beta)c$
with $c = (0,1,1,1)'$.

### 12.2 Horizon collapse at $h = 1$

At $h=1$, (8) gives $RV_{t,t+1} = RV_{t+1}$ and (12) gives $y_{t,t+1} = \ln RV_{t+1}$: both
specifications reduce to a one-step-ahead regression, the overlap term
$L_{\text{overlap}}=0$ vanishes, and log-of-mean and mean-of-logs coincide. Any discrepancy
between the two aggregation conventions at $h=1$ indicates a coding error, which makes
$h=1$ a useful unit test.

### 12.3 Scale equivariance

Rescale $RV \to c\,RV$ for $c>0$ (e.g. variance in decimals $\to$ percentage-squared, or
annualisation). In the raw specification $\beta_0 \to c\beta_0$ while
$\beta_d,\beta_w,\beta_m$ are unchanged; MSE scales by $c^2$, MAE by $c$, and QLIKE and
$R^2$ are invariant. In the log specification $\beta_0 \to \beta_0 + (1 - \beta_d - \beta_w
- \beta_m)\ln c$ with slopes unchanged, and MSE/MAE in log units are invariant. Loss
*levels* are therefore not comparable across papers unless units are stated; loss
*ratios* and QLIKE are.

## 13. Standard extensions (specify only what you estimate)

All keep the OLS-plus-HAC machinery of §7 and change only the regressor set.

**HAR-J** — adds a jump component. With bipower variation
$BPV_t = \frac{\pi}{2}\frac{M}{M-1}\sum_{j=2}^{M}|r_{t,j}||r_{t,j-1}|$ (a jump-robust
estimator of $IV_t$), the jump measure is $J_t = \max(RV_t - BPV_t,\, 0)$ and

$$
RV_{t,t+h} = \beta_0 + \beta_d RV^{(d)}_t + \beta_w RV^{(w)}_t + \beta_m RV^{(m)}_t
+ \beta_j J_t + \varepsilon^{(h)}_{t+h}.
\tag{32}
$$

**CHAR / HAR-CJ** (Andersen, Bollerslev & Diebold, 2007) — replaces $RV$ by its continuous
part $C_t$ throughout, with $J_t$ entering separately, the jump identified by a significance
test (e.g. the $Z$-statistic of Barndorff-Nielsen & Shephard) rather than by truncation
at zero.

**LHAR** (Corsi & Renò, 2012) — adds leverage via negative-return aggregates
$r^-_t{}^{(\cdot)}$, capturing the asymmetric response of volatility to negative returns.

**SHAR** (Patton & Sheppard, 2015) — splits $RV$ into signed semivariances,
$RV^{+}_t = \sum_j r_{t,j}^2 \mathbf{1}\{r_{t,j}>0\}$ and
$RV^{-}_t = \sum_j r_{t,j}^2 \mathbf{1}\{r_{t,j}<0\}$, with $RV^+_t + RV^-_t = RV_t$.

**HARQ** (Bollerslev, Patton & Quaedvlieg, 2016) — makes the daily coefficient
time-varying in the measurement error of $RV_t$, using realized quarticity
$RQ_t = \frac{M}{3}\sum_{j=1}^{M} r_{t,j}^4$:

$$
RV_{t,t+h} = \beta_0 + \big(\beta_d + \beta_{d,Q}\,RQ_t^{1/2}\big) RV^{(d)}_t
+ \beta_w RV^{(w)}_t + \beta_m RV^{(m)}_t + \varepsilon^{(h)}_{t+h}.
\tag{33}
$$

## 14. Assumptions, stated for the record

1. **Consistency of the proxy.** $RV_t$ is a consistent, conditionally unbiased estimator
   of $QV_t$; measurement error from finite $M$, microstructure noise and non-synchronous
   trading is ignored in (9)/(13). HARQ (33) is the standard response.
2. **Linearity.** The conditional mean is linear in the three components. This is an
   approximation to the cascade (7), not an implication of it.
3. **Weak dependence and stationarity.** $\{RV_t\}$ is covariance-stationary and mixing,
   giving $\hat\beta \xrightarrow{p} \beta$ and asymptotic normality under (21). Realized
   volatility is highly persistent, so this is the assumption most exposed to challenge;
   report an ADF or KPSS test on $RV_t$ and $\ln RV_t$ and note that unit-root tests have
   low power against near-long-memory alternatives.
4. **Parameter constancy.** Coefficients are constant over the estimation window (fixed
   scheme, §8.1). Structural breaks around crises are common; a rolling-window
   re-estimation is the standard robustness check.
5. **Conditional log-normality** — required only for the Jensen correction (16), not for
   estimation or for the log-scale losses.

## 16. Numerical verification

The analytical claims above are checked on the shipped EUR/USD sample by
[`docs/verify_identities.py`](verify_identities.py), which uses only the Python standard
library and so cannot inherit an error from the estimation stack:

```
python docs/verify_identities.py
```

Sample: 2012-01-12 to 2026-07-21, $T = 3{,}766$ positive-RV days, $3{,}364$ training rows.

| Claim | Result |
|---|---|
| (10) $T_h = T - h - 21$ | exact at $h = 1, 5, 22$ — $3{,}744 / 3{,}740 / 3{,}723$ |
| (15) $\exp(y_{t,t+h}) = RV_{t,t+h}$ | max relative deviation $2.9\times10^{-16}$ (machine precision) |
| (14) Jensen gap $\ge 0$ | weekly window: mean $0.081$, max $0.982$ log units — arithmetic mean up to $2.67\times$ the geometric mean; monthly: mean $0.124$, max $0.755$ |
| (30) HAR $\equiv$ constrained AR(22) | max absolute deviation $8.9\times10^{-16}$; $\sum_j\phi_j = \beta_d+\beta_w+\beta_m$ exactly |
| (23) bandwidth $L$ | $h=1$: $T_h = 3{,}342$, $L = 8$ (plug-in binds); $h=5$: $L = 8$ (both rules give 8); $h=22$: $L = 42$ (overlap binds) |

Two points are worth carrying into a write-up. First, the Jensen gap is **not** a rounding
detail: on this sample the arithmetic mean of the weekly window exceeds the geometric mean
by a factor of up to $2.67$, so the choice between (11)–(12) and mean-of-logs materially
changes the object being forecast. Second, at $h=5$ the two bandwidth rules of (22) happen
to coincide at $L=8$ on this sample, so rule (23) is only visibly binding at $h=1$ (where
the plug-in rescues an otherwise non-HAC covariance) and at $h=22$ (where the overlap floor
dominates).
